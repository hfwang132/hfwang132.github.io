> This article was written on January 18, 2020.

Today, I learned about a book called *Good to Great*. The author says:

> Good is the enemy of great. Few people attain great lives, in large part because it's just so easy to settle for a good life.

Seeing these words suddenly gave me a great deal to reflect on. Reaching the realm of “Great” requires tremendous effort and sacrifice, but if one merely wants to reach the realm of “Good,” it may be enough simply to do one's proper duties well most of the time.

I consider myself not to have achieved “Great” in my studies. During my final year of high school, I was very relaxed: every night I binge-watched shows and played PUBG—somehow, I always seemed to be just short of that final push, just lacking a bit of effort. Sometimes I would think: it is not that I am incapable of working hard; I simply do not want to work hard. But is that really the case? If someone never wants to work hard, how is that any different from being incapable of working hard? Once, during a class meeting in my final year of high school, our experimental class invited Principal Huang to give a speech. As I recall, he spoke about this issue as well, but I was rather naive at the time.

Many teachers say that the final year of high school is the time most worth working hard, because all effort has a clear purpose and is certain to be rewarded. I feel that, after entering society, since effort may not necessarily be rewarded, one may become more likely to fall into the “trap” of “Good.”

Today, a younger schoolmate asked me: Whenever I pass by the windows of other classrooms after school, I can see people still striving hard at their desks. How do those very diligent people manage to do it? I do not think I am qualified to answer that question.

---

But then again, is “Good” really so bad? At least it is not “Bad,” right? Is it wrong to spend one's life steadily and securely in a “Good” way? Clearly not.

“Good” and “Great,” in the final analysis, are merely choices between two attitudes toward life; neither is better or worse than the other. How can sparrows know the ambitions of swans? Perhaps it is not that sparrows are ignorant, but that they believe: “I leap up, only to come down after a few fathoms; I soar among the mugwort and brambles—this is the utmost of flying. And where, then, is that other one going?” This is precisely the distinction between the small and the great.

Fletcher, the band instructor in *Whiplash*, tells a story:

> After Charlie Parker messed up a performance, Jo Jones threw a cymbal at his head, and Charlie Parker later became the greatest saxophonist of all time.
> 
> However, if Jo Jones had merely said, “Well, that's okay, good job,” then the outcome would likely have been completely different.
> 
> So: “There are no two words in the English language more harmful than good job.”

But must everything be taken to such an extreme? Fletcher took that extreme path, yet he did not produce a genius like Charlie Parker. Worse still, he was reported by students' parents for abusing students and was ultimately dismissed by the music conservatory.

---

I think that, much of the time, “Good” is not the enemy of “Great”; they merely lie on a spectrum (Spectrum).

I am unwilling to sacrifice too much of my life to achieve absolute “Great.” I only hope to get as close to “Great” as possible.
