> This article was written on January 5, 2020. Looking back on it today, I feel that my freshman self was constantly pondering “ultimate” questions, really quite immersed in grand narratives. Yet I cannot help but envy that kind of youthful vigor, because the biggest problem I face every day now is what to eat for lunch.

There are simply too many interesting things in this world, yet one person seemingly does not have enough time to understand and master them one by one. Must one choose to do only one thing well in a lifetime? There are too many instruments I want to play, mathematical and physical theories I want to understand still have to wait, and before I have finished filling in one hole, I am already digging a new one. Can a person's interests not all be fully satisfied at the same time? I have heard that Poincaré was the last mathematical polymath; after him, no one was able to master mathematics and physics simultaneously. Perhaps this is because the frontier fields of science have become increasingly fragmented?

Have modern people's circles not also become increasingly fragmented? Even within music, the fragmentation of language is unimaginable: jazz guitarists often complain that jazz is “a marketless genre” (having no market)—behind the cynical indignation and helpless self-mockery lies the indifference of people from different circles: no one understands anyone else's language, and no one wants to pay attention to anyone else. If someone wishes to communicate across multiple circles, it becomes difficult for them to reach deeper territories in each one. If this continues, would they not become physically and mentally exhausted, at a loss as to what to do? In the science-fiction novel *The Ruins of Time*, people a million years in the future have long since gathered into various circles, large and small. No one pays any attention to those outside their circle; everyone is merely absorbed in their own world. Could this become humanity's bad ending?

This shows the importance of a liberal-arts education that cultivates polymaths, but polymaths ultimately cannot match specialists within a single field. Yet if the contribution of specialists is a skyscraper soaring into the clouds, then perhaps the contribution of polymaths is the complex transportation network beneath the high-rises. Thus, in their future contributions to human knowledge, polymaths sacrifice the opportunity to explore the very frontier, while repairing and connecting knowledge across humanity's different fields. Perhaps this is precisely what we need: we need polymaths to break down the barriers between circles.

However, viewed optimistically, being both a polymath and a specialist is not necessarily beyond the reach of one person, nor is the human lifespan necessarily finite. The intellectual collective in the American comic *Rick and Morty* may well become reality in the (distant) future.

The future is infinite. I hope that one day, we may devote infinite lives to an infinite world.
